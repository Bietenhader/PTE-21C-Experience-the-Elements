Aluminium is a light, silvery metal and the most abundant metal in Earth's crust; we meet it as drink cans, kitchen foil, window frames and aircraft skins. It looks reactive on paper, yet it barely corrodes, because a thin, transparent oxide film seals its surface within moments of exposure to air. Its radioactive isotope aluminium-26, with a half-life of about 717,000 years, was a major heat source that melted some of the first small planetary bodies of the young Solar System. The atom's single 3p electron ([Ne] 3s² 3p¹, ground term ²P°₁/₂) is easier to remove than magnesium's outer 3s electron: 578 kJ/mol against 738 kJ/mol.
