Sulfur is a bright-yellow nonmetal, the 'brimstone' of old texts, found around volcanic vents and in large underground deposits; it is an essential part of proteins and of the sharp garlic and mustard compounds that plants make to defend themselves. Pure sulfur hardly smells; the rotten-egg odour belongs to hydrogen sulfide. Melted, it is a runny yellow liquid, but heated further it suddenly turns dark red and as thick as treacle, because its eight-atom rings break open and link into long chains. The atom, [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴ (ground term ³P₂), has two unpaired 3p electrons, and nature provides four stable isotopes, mostly sulfur-32.
