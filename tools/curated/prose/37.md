Rubidium is a soft, silvery alkali metal that melts at 312.46 K (about 39 °C) and bursts into flame in air. It was discovered in 1861 by examining with a prism the light of a mineral heated in a flame, and named from the Latin for 'deep red' after two red lines in its spectrum. Over a quarter of natural rubidium, 27.83 %, is radioactive rubidium-87, decaying so slowly, with a half-life of 4.97 × 10¹⁰ years, that geologists use it to date ancient rocks. The atom, [Kr] 5s¹ (ground term ²S₁/₂), has one outer electron whose two hyperfine levels set the tick of rubidium atomic clocks, and rubidium-87 formed the first Bose–Einstein condensate.
