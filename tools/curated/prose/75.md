Rhenium is one of the rarest elements in Earth’s crust, a dense, silvery transition metal recovered mainly from the flue dust of molybdenum roasting. Discovered in 1925 and named after the river Rhine, it was the last element with a stable isotope to be found. Most of it now goes into nickel superalloys for jet-engine turbine blades, which must endure intense heat for years. Curiously, most natural rhenium is radioactive: rhenium-187, about 63 per cent of the total, decays to osmium-187 with a half-life of 4.33 × 10¹⁰ years, a clock used to date meteorites and ore deposits. Only rhenium-185 is stable.
